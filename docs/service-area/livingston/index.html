Livingston is the Park County seat, where Interstate 90 meets US-89. The Yellowstone River runs through town, and Paradise Valley opens to the south along that highway. Wind is the local constant. It knocks a loose note out of a story and it also finds every seal a cooling system has.

Vehicles here sit in alley spots behind Main Street shops, in yards off the frontage, and on ranch roads that drop toward the river. A Paradise Valley address can be a mile marker on US-89 rather than a street. Write the marker. A pin still helps when the ranch road leaves the highway at a cattle guard with no sign.

River wind and winter cold make no-crank mornings ordinary. Describe the sound: click, slow roll, or silence. Brakes that pulled after the drive down from a trailhead south of town are a different note from a battery note. Keep them separate so the visit starts at the right end of the vehicle.

The rail and highway corridors mean work trucks overnight within earshot of I-90 and then leave at dawn. If a fleet unit will only be in the yard until a certain window, say so. The public board cannot reserve today or tomorrow. The phone can.

Paradise Valley trailheads feed backcountry texts. Those belong with a location pin and the vehicle's symptom, sent as a text, not as a hope that a street address will appear. In town, a normal block number is plenty.

Livingston is not a suburb page for Bozeman. The river, US-89, and the valley south of it are the geography that changes the meeting place. Use them in the note. A block downtown and a mile marker in Paradise Valley should never share one vague sentence. Name the block, the marker, or the ranch road, and stop there. A second landmark is useful only when the first one is a highway.
