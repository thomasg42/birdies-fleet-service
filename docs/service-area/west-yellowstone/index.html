West Yellowstone sits in Gallatin County at the West Entrance of Yellowstone, where US-20, US-191, and US-287 meet. Hebgen Lake is to the west, along the highway people use when the park road is not the plan. Summer streets are full. Shoulder seasons are quiet and cold. Both change how a disabled vehicle should be described.

A truck at the entrance is not a truck at the lake. The entrance has rangers, lines, and short tempers. The lake has pullouts, boat ramps, and long gaps with no building name. Write which one, then add the pin. If you are inside the park, say that plainly, because a meeting place at the gate and a meeting place at a trailhead are not interchangeable.

Visitor vehicles die in hotel lots after a night of lights and a heater fan. Local work trucks die in the yard behind a shop on the edge of town. Snow machines and summer trailers come and go, but the booking is for the vehicle that will not move: make, simple description, and the fault. No crank, a grind from a corner, coolant on the ground, or a lamp on the dash.

Hebgen Lake wind and early cold make battery and charging complaints common in the shoulder season. Say whether the vehicle sat for a night or a week. A week at a cabin lot is a different story from a stall at a fuel island.

Forest roads around the town feed backcountry texts. Mile markers on US-191 and US-20 are useful when the pin is slow to load. Name the highway you are actually on. Three highways meet here, and "the main road" is not one of them.

West Yellowstone is listed on its own because the park entrance creates meeting places that do not exist in Bozeman or Big Sky. Use the entrance, the lake, or the town yard as the first words of the note.
